Fear

A.Br., woman of 28, right side. A probe gave her terror for an instant, of the kind she had in her attacks. Probes at other points in the same patient gave singing. [Case 30, pp. 641–642]

More from the 1963 case reports

C23
R.M., man of 31, left side. Under the probe he felt very scared and said this feeling was his attack. Thirteen minutes later a probe at the same point made him say he was going to die. [Case 23, pp. 635–636]
C15
J.V., girl of 14, right side. While the probe made her hear voices shouting at her, she felt that something dreadful was about to happen. [Case 15, pp. 629–630]

Paraphrased from the case reports of Penfield and Perot 1963. A probe is a touch of the stimulating electrode. The numbers are the case numbers of that paper.

Term, count and site

In the case reports of the 1963 paper fear also comes with some memory-like scenes. The authors write that many patients were aware of pleasure or fear attached to what came back.

Penfield’s term Interpretive illusion of emotion

Count The 1963 paper says the emotional group was too small to analyse, except for fear.

Where Either temporal region, by the caption of Fig. 1. The figure does not letter a band for fear, and this plate shades the whole lobe.

Side Either side.

Later evidence

HIST
In 1959 Penfield offered “interpretive cortex” as a working name for parts of both temporal lobes where a gentle current sometimes gave one of two responses: a vivid return of a past experience, or a sudden change in how the present felt. [Penfield 1959]
HIST
In a lecture of 1957 Penfield listed the feelings that stimulation could produce as fear, loneliness, separation, sorrow and disgust. He gave no counts. [Penfield 1958]
STIM
In 74 epilepsy patients, 79 of 938 stimulations of the temporal lobe produced an emotion, and in 67 of the 79 it was fear. Fear came most often from the amygdala, and women reported it more often than men. [Meletti et al. 2006]
STIM
In eight epilepsy patients, about half of the brief stimulations of the amygdala changed how the patient felt. On the right the feeling was always unpleasant: fear, anxiety or sadness. On the left it was unpleasant about half the time and happy the rest. Five of the eight had an electrode on the right and five on the left. [Lanteaume et al. 2007]
STIM
In nine epilepsy patients, stimulating the amygdala reliably changed sweating and heart rate, and seven of the nine felt no emotion. One man felt fear that grew to terror as the voltage on his right amygdala was raised. [Inman et al. 2020]
