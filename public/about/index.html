About

Wilder Penfield

Wilder Graves Penfield (1891 to 1976) was a neurosurgeon. He was born in Spokane, Washington, studied at Princeton and at Oxford, where Charles Sherrington introduced him to the study of the brain, and took his medical degree at Johns Hopkins in 1918. In 1928 he moved from New York to the Royal Victoria Hospital in Montreal with his surgical partner William Cone. In 1934 he founded the Montreal Neurological Institute. He directed it until 1960 and died in Montreal.

His subject was epilepsy. He operated under local anaesthetic, so the patient was conscious. He stimulated points on the exposed cortex with an electric current and recorded what the patient reported. The aim was to find and remove the tissue where the seizures began. This way of operating became known as the Montreal procedure. The responses on the experience map were recorded during those operations. The body map is a by-product of those operations and of some tumour operations in which he mapped the cortex in the same way.

Sources: Feindel, Canadian Encyclopedia. The Neuro, McGill. McGill 200. Penfield & Boldrey 1937.

The two maps

The experience map covers what his patients heard, saw, remembered and felt when the temporal lobe was stimulated, with later work on feeling from other parts of the brain.

The body map covers the strip of cortex for touch and movement, the figure often called the homunculus.

How the maps were made and checked is under Methods. The sources are under References. Every record also has a page of its own: index of records.

The site is a research companion to NEURAL, a book by Velco Dar on brain-computer interfaces forthcoming from Fast Company Press in 2027.

The experience map

What the temporal lobe gave

On the sensorimotor strip a current gives a movement or a tingling in some part of the body. That is the subject of the Body map. On the anterior transverse gyrus of Heschl it gives a crude sound such as buzzing. Elsewhere in the temporal lobe Penfield sometimes got something of another kind. A patient heard a voice or a piece of music, or saw people or a place, and often knew it as something from the past. He called this an experiential response. He first reported one in 1938. In 1963, with Phanor Perot, he published a final summary of every case.

That paper reviews 1,288 consecutive operations on 1,132 patients. The temporal lobe was explored and stimulated in 520 of them. Forty reported a memory-like experience under the electrode. Among the 612 patients explored elsewhere, none did.

The stimulus usually used was a train of square-wave pulses, each of 2 to 5 milliseconds, 40 to 100 a second, at 1 to 5 volts. The patients stayed aware of the operating room while the experience ran. Where it could be tested, the experience moved forward at a normal pace while the current was on and stopped when the electrode was lifted. Occasionally it outlasted the stimulus.

A second kind of response brought nothing back. It changed how the present seemed: familiar, strange, louder, nearer, frightening. The paper calls these interpretive illusions, and it calls the cortex that gave both kinds of response the interpretive cortex.

Some kinds of experience never came. The paper lists them: making up one’s mind, skilled acts, speaking, writing, adding figures, eating and tasting, sexual experience, painful suffering and weeping.

The authors do not claim that the temporal cortex holds the record of the past. They suggest that the record lies in circuits at a distance, and that this cortex can call it up.

Sources: Penfield & Perot 1963. Penfield 1938, as cited there.

What later work changed

Groups that worked later with electrodes placed deep in the brain moved the source of these experiences inward. In one series of 29 patients, dream-like experiences came only when a seizure or the current reached the limbic structures deep in the temporal lobe, and the outer cortex did not have to take part. In another series a dreamy state could be started from the amygdala, from the hippocampus or from the outer cortex, and a larger share of the deep sites produced it.

A review of 80 years of reports counted 273 stimulations that brought back a memory. Most of the memories were vague, about 14% were of one particular event, and well under 1% of stimulations produced one.

Some doubt that these are memories at all. Loftus and Loftus read Penfield and Perot’s case notes and argued that even the most lifelike reports look like scenes the patient put together. Sjöberg argues that the patient may take a mental event for a memory, and says that neither account can yet be proved.

A map of 1,537 stimulated sites in 67 patients shows how unevenly the brain answers. Stimulation produced a noticeable effect at just over half the sites in the networks for movement, touch and vision. In the limbic and default networks it did so at about a fifth to a quarter of the sites, and the effects there were more varied and included emotion and recall.

Sources: Gloor et al. 1982. Bancaud et al. 1994. Curot et al. 2017. Loftus & Loftus 1980. Sjöberg 2023. Fox et al. 2020.

Significance to brain-computer interfaces

Reading
A song has been rebuilt from recordings made over the convolution where Penfield’s patients heard music. Twenty-nine patients listened to the song while their brain activity was recorded. Nothing was stimulated, and what was read out was a song being heard, not one imagined. Record: Music
Memory
In 25 epilepsy patients a system watched brain activity while they learned lists of words, and gave a half-second burst of stimulation to the lateral temporal cortex when it predicted that a word would be forgotten. Words stimulated in this way were about 15% more likely to be recalled. The authors call it a proof of concept.
Mood
One woman with severe depression was given a device that answered a signal in her amygdala with six seconds of stimulation. Her depression score fell by more than half in 12 days. In a study of 25 epilepsy patients, 100 to 200 seconds of stimulation of the lateral orbitofrontal cortex lifted mood in those with moderate to severe symptoms of depression. Record: Mood
Preference
One man’s taste in music changed while a stimulator in his nucleus accumbens was running, and changed back when its batteries ran down. Record: A changed preference
Limits
Nearly every finding on the experience map comes from people with epilepsy, or with another illness that was the reason for the electrodes. Several rest on one patient. Most stimulations produce no experience. In nine patients whose amygdala was stimulated, the body answered reliably and seven of them felt no emotion. Record: Fear

Sources: Bellier et al. 2023. Ezzyat et al. 2018. Scangos et al. 2021. Rao et al. 2018. Mantione et al. 2014. Inman et al. 2020.

The body map

The 1937 and 1950 figures

In 1937 Penfield and Edwin Boldrey pooled the stimulation records of 126 operations. Movements and sensations of the parts of the body came from the two gyri beside the central sulcus in a regular order, from the lower limb at the medial end to the face, tongue and throat at the lateral end. They summarised the order and the relative extent of each part in a drawing of a distorted human figure, the homunculus. For the 1950 book with Theodore Rasmussen the drawing was redone as two figures laid on a cross section of the hemisphere, one for sensation along the postcentral gyrus and one for movement along the precentral gyrus. By then the series had grown to about 400 operations.

The parts do not get equal room. By the block lines of the 1950 sensory figure, as measured by Wright, the hand and its five digits take 19% of the total line length, the three lip labels 14% and the tongue 9%, against 2% for the trunk. On the motor figure the hand and digits take 26%, the lips 15% and the trunk 2%.

Penfield and Rasmussen called the motor figure an aid to memory and wrote that scientific accuracy in such a drawing is impossible. Later work has kept the broad order and revised the detail. A commentary on a stimulation series of 100 patients published in 2020 describes considerable overlap between neighbouring parts of the upper limb, and no discrete zones of the kind drawn in 1950. A 2023 imaging study divides the motor strip into three effector regions that alternate with three inter-effector regions. Each record on the body map lists the revisions for its label.

Sources: Penfield & Boldrey 1937. Penfield & Rasmussen 1950. Wright 1956. Catani 2017. Roux et al. 2020. Schieber 2020. Gordon et al. 2023.

Significance to brain-computer interfaces

Most of the implants in the studies below sit in the hand or mouth territory of the strip, or over it. The 2026 recordings also sampled the middle of the precentral gyrus, one speech study also had arrays in area 44, and the amputation study used fMRI without an implant.

Reading movement
A paper published in 2006 reported that an array in the arm and hand area let a man with tetraplegia move a cursor and open and close a prosthetic hand. A paper published in 2021 reported that two arrays in the hand knob of the precentral gyrus decoded attempted handwriting at 90 characters per minute. Record: Hand, motor figure
Reading speech
Two papers published in 2023 reported speech decoding. Arrays on the ventral precentral gyrus decoded attempted speech at 62 words per minute, and a surface grid over sensorimotor and temporal speech cortex decoded text at a median of 78 words per minute. Record: Tongue, motor figure
Writing sensation
Penfield’s patients felt tingling in a part of the body when the postcentral gyrus was stimulated. Microstimulation through implanted arrays in the hand region of the same gyrus evoked touch that a man with spinal cord injury felt as coming from his own hand. With that feedback his median time for a successful trial on a clinical arm-function test with a robotic arm was 10.2 s, against 20.9 s without it. Record: Index finger, sensory figure
After injury
Three adults scanned before and after arm amputation kept stable hand and lip maps for as long as they were followed, which was five years in one case. Record: Hand, sensory figure
Limits of the map
The 1950 figure draws one body part per zone. Recordings from 20 arrays in 8 people found the whole body represented at every sampled site on the crown of the precentral gyrus, with relative strength roughly following the homunculus. The authors of the handwriting study call their hand knob site premotor, and in the 2023 speech study that used implanted arrays two further arrays in area 44 carried almost no speech information. Record: Hand, motor figure Record: Tongue, motor figure
