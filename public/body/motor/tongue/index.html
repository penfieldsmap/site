Under stimulation

DES
Face movements, tongue included, were evoked in 52 patients. The authors describe a medial-to-lateral sequence of forehead, eyebrows, eyes, nose, cheeks, both lips and chin. Responses above the lips were few (forehead 2, eyebrow 1, eyelid 2, nostril 1, cheek 11). [Roux et al. 2020] Applies to: motor figure: face and mouth
DES
The 2020 series counted 38 lip, 35 tongue and 12 jaw responses. In the 11 patients with both lip and tongue sites, the order from medial to lateral was upper lip, lower lip, tongue. [Roux et al. 2020] Applies to: motor figure: mouth
Not specific to this label
DES
Penfield wrote that when he made a conscious patient move his hand by stimulating the motor cortex and asked him about it, the reply was invariably: “I didn’t do that. You did.” (p. 76). [Penfield 1975] Applies to: all motor labels
DES
In a series of 100 patients published in 2020, 608 sites were stimulated in the precentral gyrus and 1,937 in the gyri nearby. A movement followed at 248 sites, 245 of them in the precentral gyrus. The mean threshold was 2.1 mA. Movements were basic and stereotyped, and were fast without the profile of a natural movement. Flexions of the wrist and digits were far more frequent than extensions. No complex action, such as bringing food to the mouth, was seen. [Roux et al. 2020] Applies to: all motor labels
DES
Responses in the 2020 series were judged from the patient’s report and the team’s observation, without EMG. 59% of precentral sites gave no response, and the part of area 4 buried in the central sulcus could not be reached. [Roux et al. 2020] Applies to: all motor labels

Injury, disease and surgery

CLIN
Infarcts in the territory of the middle cerebral artery, which supplies most of the lateral surface, typically cause weakness that is worse in the opposite arm and face than in the leg. Sensory loss mainly affects the face and arm. Occlusion of the main stem causes weakness of the whole side. [Lui et al., StatPearls; MSD Manual; Margetis & Sánchez-Manso, StatPearls] Applies to: arm, hand, face and mouth labels, both figures
Not specific to this label
CLIN
Focal motor seizures can spread through neighbouring body parts in the order of the motor map. This is the Jacksonian march. Bravais described such seizures in 1827. Hughlings Jackson described the march between 1868 and 1870 and inferred that body parts are represented in separate parts of the brain. Penfield and Boldrey published their stimulation map in 1937. A 2024 series found that spread is not explained by adjacency alone. [Eadie 2010; York & Steinberg 2007; York & Steinberg 2011; Fotedar et al. 2024; Penfield & Boldrey 1937] Applies to: all motor labels

Neural interfaces

REC
Arrays in area 6v (ventral premotor cortex, on the ventral precentral gyrus) decoded the attempted speech of a woman with ALS at 62 words per minute. Two further arrays in area 44 carried almost no speech information. [Willett et al. 2023] Applies to: motor figure: mouth; function brackets
REC
A surface electrode grid over the sensorimotor and temporal speech cortex of a woman paralysed by a brainstem stroke decoded text at a median of 78 words per minute. [Metzger et al. 2023] Applies to: motor figure: mouth; function brackets

Revisions and disputes

DES
A commentary on the 2020 data describes, for the upper limb and the head, a gradual progression with overlap between neighbouring parts, and no discrete zones of the kind drawn in 1950. The study’s authors use the term relative somatotopy for the elbow, wrist and hand region. [Schieber 2020; Roux et al. 2020] Applies to: motor figure: upper limb, hand, face and mouth
DES
According to the 2020 authors, Penfield described vocalization, salivation and mastication from the lips, jaw and tongue zone. None of the three was seen in their series. The 1950 text says that vocalization has no fixed place in the sequence and that salivation was more often a postcentral than a precentral response. [Roux et al. 2020; Penfield & Rasmussen 1950] Applies to: motor figure: mouth; function brackets
fMRI
In the mouth effector region the tongue is central and the jaw more peripheral, with the larynx at the perimeter. The paper’s schematic also draws the eyes at the outer edge of this region. [Gordon et al. 2023] Applies to: motor figure: jaw and tongue
Not specific to this label
DES
According to the 2020 authors, Penfield found about 20% of motor responses outside the precentral gyrus, particularly in the postcentral gyrus. In their own series, 3 of 1,937 sites stimulated outside the precentral gyrus produced movement. [Roux et al. 2020] Applies to: all motor labels
ANAT
Primary motor cortex (area 4) lies mostly in the anterior bank of the central sulcus. Near the midline it widens onto the gyral surface and the paracentral lobule. Much of the exposed crown of the precentral gyrus is premotor cortex (area 6). [White et al. 1997; Deo et al. 2026] Applies to: all motor labels
fMRI
Precision fMRI in 7 intensively scanned adults, replicated in group-averaged datasets of about 4,000, 3,928 and 812 people, divides the precentral gyrus into three effector regions (foot, hand, mouth) that alternate with three inter-effector regions. See column C of Fig. 2. [Gordon et al. 2023] Applies to: all motor labels
REC
Single-neuron recordings from 20 microelectrode arrays in 8 people found the whole body represented at every sampled site on the crown of the precentral gyrus, with relative strength roughly following the homunculus. [Deo et al. 2026] Applies to: all motor labels; 2023 regions

The 1950 figures

HIST
Catani (2017) judges the tongue exaggerated in the 1937 figure and corrected in the 1950 figures. [Catani 2017] Applies to: tongue, both figures
Not specific to this label
HIST
The 1937 figure rested on 126 usable operation records out of 163 operations done under local anaesthesia from 1928 to 1936. Boldrey compiled 170 summary charts, one for each movement or sensation. The 1950 figures drew on about 400 craniotomies, including a second series from 1936 to 1947. [Penfield & Boldrey 1937; Penfield & Rasmussen 1950; Catani 2017] Applies to: all 1950 labels
HIST
Hortense Pauline Cantlie drew the homunculus. One combined figure appeared in the 1937 paper. For the 1950 book she drew two new figures, one sensory (Fig. 17) and one motor (Fig. 22). In the reproductions viewed, both drawings carry her monogram and the year 1948. [Penfield & Rasmussen 1950; Pogliano 2012; Stuart et al. 1951] Applies to: all 1950 labels
HIST
Penfield and Rasmussen wrote that the motor homunculus may be used as an aid to memory and called it “a cartoon of representation in which scientific accuracy is impossible” (p. 56). Of the sensory homunculus they wrote that such drawings become confusing if too much significance is attributed to shape and comparative size, and that the indication of extent under the figure corrects this (p. 214). [Penfield & Rasmussen 1950] Applies to: all 1950 labels
HIST
Every body-part label on the 1950 plates except Gen. has a block line along the cortex. The three function words on the motor figure are brackets and have no line. The legend of the sensory figure says the length of these lines shows the comparative extent of each representation more accurately than the drawing. Wright (1956) measured every line, 28 on the sensory figure and 22 on the motor figure. The legend of the motor figure does not repeat the statement. [Penfield & Rasmussen 1950; Wright 1956] Applies to: all 1950 labels
HIST
Penfield and Boldrey scaled the 1937 figure by the vertical extent of each part along the central sulcus, without statistics. A 2017 reanalysis found that the figure was not in proportion to the paper’s own measurements. Stimulus intensity, number of patients, number of stimulations per patient and overlap between parts were not taken into account. The reanalysed sensory maps overlap substantially, mostly near the central sulcus. [Penfield & Boldrey 1937; Catani 2017] Applies to: all 1950 labels
HIST
The 1937 figure was a single drawing without labels, not laid on the brain. The 1950 figures are each laid on the outline of a coronal section, with named labels and block lines. The sensory figure adds the genitals and an intra-abdominal field. The motor figure adds three function brackets. [Penfield & Boldrey 1937; Penfield & Rasmussen 1950] Applies to: all 1950 labels
